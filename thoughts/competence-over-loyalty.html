Why Competence Matters More Than Blind Loyalty

Home Thoughts
August 1, 2026

I've been watching the news lately, and it's hard not to notice a major shift in how political organizations are choosing their leaders. More and more, loyalty to one specific person is being treated as the ultimate test, while actual skill, expertise, and a respect for the rule of law take a back seat.

That's a dangerous game. When any political party swaps out real governing ability for total obedience, it eventually hits a wall. You can win short-term political fights with pure loyalty, but you can't run a country on it.

🧪 The Loyalty Test

In March 2024, after Donald Trump's team took leadership of the Republican National Committee, existing staff were required to re-apply for their jobs. During the re-interview process, applicants were directly asked: "Was the 2020 election stolen?"

Think about that for a moment. Not "What's your policy expertise?" or "What governing experience do you bring?" The single most important question was whether you agreed with one specific claim. Applicants who acknowledged the certified election results were routinely filtered out. Every role—from researchers and lawyers to general staff—was subject to this single-issue loyalty screening.

By August 2026, a Politico investigation into the Heritage Foundation revealed the downstream effect of this approach: institutions that are supposed to identify and train the party's future governing class have spent the last decade selecting for rigid conformity over more typical markers of expertise. As traditional conservatives inside and outside government have warned, this produces political operatives who are skilled at enforcing the party line but ill-equipped to handle the actual mechanics of governance.

🏛️ Brain Drain and Institutional Decay

When institutions replace traditional markers of merit—legal rigor, policy knowledge, professional qualifications—with strict ideological tests, the long-term talent pipeline deteriorates. The party ends up with people who are great at messaging but struggle when they actually have to manage a complex economy, navigate foreign policy, or run a federal agency.

Whether it's managing inflation, handling tricky foreign relations, or just keeping the basic rules of our system intact, practical skill matters. When real-world problems hit, simple slogans don't solve them.

🔄 The Binary Trap

There's another layer to this problem. In a healthy political environment, people can debate where on the spectrum between unregulated markets and smart regulation a society should land. But in a hyper-polarized environment, nuance gets thrown out the window. Any deviation from the party line gets labeled as the enemy's ideology—you're either with us, or you're a socialist.

That kind of binary framing is powerful because it's simple: explaining the trade-offs of a mixed economy takes time, but calling an opponent a "socialist" triggers an immediate emotional response. It enforces party discipline and shifts the conversation from "Is this policy actually working for people?" to "Which team are you on?"

The irony is that virtually no serious political party advocates for pure laissez-faire capitalism. Even the strongest free-market supporters generally accept the need for property rights enforcement, contract law, basic infrastructure, and national defense. But when absolute principles replace practical outcomes, any attempt to regulate or build safety nets gets treated as an attack on the system itself—even when the accusers quietly support their own brand of government intervention.

⚖️ To Be Fair

I'll be the first to admit that my perspective has blind spots. I'm an institutionalist by nature, and the electoral picture is more complicated than "loyalty = losing."

The modern GOP has made real gains with working-class voters across racial lines. Many working-class Hispanic, Black, and male voters without college degrees have responded to populist messaging on border security, economic protectionism, and cultural traditionalism. If the party successfully trades college-educated suburbanites for a broad, multiracial working-class base, that creates a formidable coalition that defies classic projections. And honestly, seeing working-class voters unite across racial lines is a genuinely positive development, regardless of party.

On top of that, the U.S. constitutional framework—the Electoral College, the Senate, state legislative maps—gives outsized weight to smaller, rural, and less densely populated states. Even if urban corridors lean heavily away from the GOP, geographic distribution keeps the party competitive regardless of popular vote totals. And negative partisanship—voters punishing the party in power out of frustration rather than deep love for the alternative—makes it extremely difficult for either party to be relegated to a multi-generational minority.

📉 But Promises Meet Reality

Here's the thing, though: when working-class voters cross party lines, they generally do so expecting immediate, tangible relief on living costs. And halfway through 2026, the picture is mixed at best.

While the administration promised a broad revival in blue-collar manufacturing through aggressive tariffs, the domestic manufacturing sector has continued to shed jobs. Most job growth has concentrated in infrastructure and tech-construction sectors like AI data centers—leaving many traditional working-class workers feeling little relief. Meanwhile, broad inflation has hovered around 3.5%, and essential living costs—especially energy and gas prices—have spiked, driven heavily by ongoing Middle East conflict and Strait of Hormuz shipping disruptions.

And that conflict itself tells its own story. What was supposed to be a decisive, short-term military operation to eliminate Iran's nuclear capabilities has stretched into months of daily bombing runs against hardened, deeply buried facilities that have proven resistant to initial strikes. A prolonged foreign conflict directly undermines the domestic economic narrative: daily air campaigns and naval blockades in the Persian Gulf shock energy markets, drive up global oil prices, and fuel the exact inflation that hits working-class voters hardest at the pump.

That presents a brutal double-edged sword for the GOP base. Isolationist populists feel betrayed because the administration got dragged into another deep Middle Eastern military commitment despite "America First" promises. And institutionalists and moderates view the prolonged campaign as proof of erratic foreign policy that lacks a clear exit strategy.

🎯 The Bottom Line

History shows us that good government exists to protect rights, enforce rules fairly, and work for the common good. Parties that forget that and demand rigid obedience over competence usually wind up pushing the broader public away in the long run.

Promising quick fixes—whether "tariffs will instantly fix manufacturing" or "a quick bombing campaign will solve nuclear proliferation"—makes for powerful campaign rhetoric. But when those policies run into economic friction and military realities, a party that filtered out expert dissent and technical skill at the door finds itself struggling to adapt.

Governing competence will always matter more than pure loyalty to one person.
